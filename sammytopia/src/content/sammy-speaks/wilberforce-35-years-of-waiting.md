# SAMMY SPEAKS
## WILBERFORCE: 35 YEARS OF WAITING

There are some victories that arrive with applause.
And there are others that arrive quietly, after years of waiting.

For my school, Wilberforce House belonged to the second kind.

For years, the name Wilberforce carried a strange feeling in our school community. It was a house with history, identity and people who were proud to belong to it. Yet there was one thing missing.

The victory.

For roughly 35 years, Wilberforce House had waited.

Year after year, other houses celebrated. Trophies moved from one place to another. Students graduated, new students arrived, and the competition continued.

But Wilberforce kept waiting.

Eventually, waiting can become part of a story.
People begin to accept it.
Maybe this is simply how things are.
Maybe the trophy belongs somewhere else.
Maybe this is just another year.

But sometimes, a group of people decides that history does not have to repeat itself.

That was the beginning of our journey.

When I became House Captain of Wilberforce House, I understood that leadership would mean more than wearing a badge or standing in front of students.

It would mean convincing people to believe in something that had not happened for decades.

We had to believe before we could win.

And belief alone was not enough.

We needed discipline.
We needed teamwork.
We needed students who were willing to give their best even when nobody could guarantee the result.

Every competition became important.
Every practice mattered.
Every point counted.

There were moments when the pressure was obvious. Sometimes the gap between victory and defeat seemed incredibly small. A single performance, a single race, a single decision could change everything.

But something was happening within the house.

Students began to believe that the story could change.

We were no longer simply participating.
We were pursuing something.

The idea of winning after 35 years became more than a trophy.
It became a question:

What happens when people stop accepting the story they inherited?

That question stayed with me.

Because leadership is not always about creating something completely new.
Sometimes it is about looking at something that has been stuck for a long time and saying:

It doesn't have to remain this way.

Our journey demanded unity.

Different students had different strengths. Some excelled in athletics. Others contributed through academics, competitions, performances or other activities.

No single person could win the competition alone.

That was perhaps the most important lesson.

A house is not one person.
It is a collection of people who decide to move in the same direction.

And gradually, Wilberforce moved.

We worked.
We competed.
We encouraged one another.
We kept going.

Then came the moment.

After roughly 35 years of waiting, Wilberforce House won.

The trophy was no longer something we could only imagine holding.
It was ours.

The celebration was powerful, but what stayed with me was not simply the trophy.
It was what the trophy represented.

Thirty-five years of waiting had finally become a story of victory.

And suddenly, the years before us looked different.

The students who had competed decades earlier were no longer simply people who had failed to win.
They were part of the history that made the eventual victory meaningful.
Their waiting had become part of our story.

That taught me something about perseverance.

Sometimes you work without seeing the result.
Sometimes you begin something that somebody else will finish.
Sometimes the person who plants the seed never gets to sit beneath the tree.

But that does not make the planting meaningless.

Wilberforce taught me that progress can take longer than we expect.
It also taught me that history is not always permanent.

A tradition can change.
A record can be broken.
A door that has remained closed for years can eventually open.

And sometimes, all it takes is someone willing to knock again.

Looking back, I realise that being House Captain was never really about being the person at the front.
It was about helping people see what was possible.

The victory belonged to everyone.

To the students who competed.
To those who encouraged.
To those who practised when nobody was watching.
To those who believed when belief was difficult.
And perhaps, in some small way, to everyone who had worn Wilberforce colours before us and kept the house alive through those years of waiting.

The trophy eventually came.

But the greater victory had already happened.

We had stopped asking whether Wilberforce could win.
We had started asking how far Wilberforce could go.

And that is the part of the story I want to remember.

Not simply that we won.
But that we believed a different ending was possible.

Thirty-five years is a long time to wait.
But sometimes, waiting is not the end of a story.
Sometimes, it is simply the chapter before the breakthrough.

— Samuel Joshua Jason Pratt

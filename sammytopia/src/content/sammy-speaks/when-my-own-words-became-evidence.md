# SAMMY SPEAKS
## WHEN MY OWN WORDS BECAME EVIDENCE

I never thought I would have to prove that my own words belonged to me.

I thought writing was simple. You sit down, find the words hiding somewhere inside your head, arrange them on a page, and hope someone understands what you were trying to say.

Then I met the AI detector.

Suddenly, writing was no longer only about what I had written. It was about whether a machine believed I had written it.

And that changed the way I looked at my own sentences.

I began asking questions I had never imagined asking myself. Does this sound too polished? Does this sentence sound too perfect? Would a machine think I wrote this? Should I change this word? Should I make this sentence simpler? Should I leave a little imperfection behind so that a computer will believe I am human?

There was something almost absurd about it. I was writing because I had something to say, yet I found myself worrying about whether a machine would approve of the way I said it.

The strange part was that I wasn't trying to disguise anything. I was trying to preserve something.

My voice.

Writing has always been more than putting grammatically correct words together for me. It is where thoughts become visible. It is where an experience can become a story and an ordinary moment can become something worth remembering.

So when an AI detector gave a piece of writing a suspicious score, the number felt strangely personal. A percentage on a screen suddenly seemed capable of questioning something I knew about myself.

But the more I thought about it, the more I realized that a detector does not know me. It doesn't know how many times I have rewritten a sentence. It doesn't know the strange phrases that naturally appear in my writing. It doesn't know the books I have read, the teachers who have corrected me, the essays I have written, the conversations that have shaped the way I communicate, or the experiences that gave me something to write about in the first place.

It sees patterns.

I live the story behind them.

That distinction matters.

Technology can be useful. Artificial intelligence can help people brainstorm, learn, organize ideas, and work more efficiently. But there is something deeply human about having an experience and deciding that it is worth putting into words.

A machine can analyse the words. It cannot live the life that produced them.

That experience changed the way I think about writing. I still care about clarity. I still care about grammar. I still want my writing to be strong. But I don't want to sand away everything that makes it mine simply to satisfy a detector.

I don't want to write for a machine.

I want to write for a person.

Someone who might read my words and recognize a thought they have had themselves. Someone who might pause at a sentence because it reminds them of something. Someone who might finish an article knowing a little more about the person behind the words.

Because at the end of the day, the most important question is not: “Did a machine think this was human?”

The question is: “Does this sound like me?”

And if it does, then perhaps that is evidence enough.

— Samuel Joshua Jason Pratt
